The classic beer pong setup is a triangle of **10 cups per team**. When a team loses cups, the triangle is pushed back together: first to **6**, then to **3** cups. That is exactly how we play, and exactly how [Pong Stats](/en/products/pong-stats) records it. This post shows what the setup looks like, when to re-rack and why custom formations are perfectly fine too.

## The standard setup: 10 cups in a triangle

Each team gets 10 cups in four rows: **4, 3, 2 and 1**. The wide row of four cups sits at the end of the table, and the tip with the single cup points towards the middle of the table. Both teams build exactly the same triangle, so both sides face the same targets.

- **Back row (4 cups):** For many players, the four cups at the end of the table are the hardest targets.
- **Middle rows (3 and 2 cups):** They narrow the triangle towards the tip.
- **Tip (1 cup):** The front cup is the closest one to the thrower.

The cups touch each other and the triangle is always symmetrical. You can read more about the game rules on our [beer pong rules](/en/rules) page.

## Re-rack: pushing the triangle back together

As cups get hit, the table gets emptier. To keep the cups reachable, we rebuild the formation. This is how we handle it:

- **10 to 7 cups left:** The setup stays and hit cups are removed.
- **6 to 4 cups left:** The cups are pushed together into a small triangle of **3, 2 and 1** (the 6-cup formation).
- **3 or 2 cups left:** The cups are arranged in a triangle of **2 and 1** (the 3-cup formation).
- **Only 1 cup left:** The last cup always stands in the middle at the end of the table.

If a team hits all the cups in the final throws at the end of a game, 3 new cups are set up for both teams, which is the 3-cup formation, and the game continues.

## Why cup position matters

Not every cup is equally hard to hit. If you always skip the back cups, or mostly hit the tip, you play by a clear pattern. For every hit, Pong Stats records the formation and the position of the cup. In Pro, this becomes a heatmap, split by 10-, 6- and 3-cup formation. That shows you which cups you hit reliably and where there is room to improve.

## Custom formations: that is beer pong too

Not every table plays 4-3-2-1, and it does not have to. Many groups set up their own formations, for example a **diamond**, **two rows** side by side or a **circle**. These setups are just as playable, they only change which cups are easy or hard to hit.

![Three examples of custom formations: diamond, two rows and a circle made of red cups](/images/news/beer-pong-eigene-aufstellungen.webp)

To keep it fair, agree on a few things before the first throw:

1. **Shape and number:** Both teams build the same formation with the same number of cups.
2. **Re-rack:** Is there a rebuild, and if so, at how many cups?
3. **Spacing:** Do the cups touch, or are they deliberately spread out?
4. **Overtime:** Which formation do you continue with once all cups are gone?

> Tip: A harder formation is a nice handicap when a strong team plays against a weaker one.
